Planning Optimal Grasps

This page is a from-scratch rewrite of Ferrari and Canny's 1992 paper. It keeps the real ideas and notation, but explains them in the order a human would want to learn them: what problem the paper solves, how it models contact, what the wrench-set geometry means, why there are two criteria, and how the planning algorithm actually uses the quality number.

Problem Model Quality Criteria Algorithm Interactive Lab

Core Idea

Turn a grasp into a set of feasible wrenches under a unit force budget, then score the grasp by the radius of the largest origin-centered ball contained in that set.

Why The Math Looks Weird

The paper compresses contact mechanics, force budgeting, and convex geometry into the same notation. Once those are separated, the symbols mostly become bookkeeping.

What To Remember

Primitive contact rays become primitive wrenches; primitive wrenches generate a feasible wrench set; the nearest face of that set determines quality.

1. What Problem The Paper Solves

The paper is about grasp planning, not just grasp execution. Suppose a robot can place its fingers on an object in several ways. Which grasp should it choose before execution? Ferrari and Canny want one number that ranks candidate grasps by how robustly they can resist unknown disturbances.

One sentence version Good grasps leave a bigger cushion around the zero-disturbance point.

Force Closure Comes First

The paper starts from grasps that already satisfy force closure. The fingers should be able to balance any external wrench without changing where the contacts are.

Force closure is the yes-or-no question.
Ferrari-Canny quality is the "how much margin do I have?" question.

What The Quality Number Means

The score is not about one favorable disturbance direction. It is the worst case over all directions, because a planner does not get to choose what disturbance the world applies later.

Larger quality means a larger full ball of disturbances can be resisted.
Zero quality means some disturbance direction touches the boundary immediately.

2. Contact Model, Wrenches, And Scaling

The paper uses the hard-finger contact model. Each finger can apply a force inside its friction cone. More complicated contacts such as edges or faces are represented using equivalent point contacts. For planar objects the resulting wrench space is three-dimensional, which is why this rewrite focuses on the planar case.

Planar wrench space Two force components plus one out-of-plane torque.

The Wrench Vector

A wrench packages force and torque into one vector. In 3D that vector has six components. In the planar case it shrinks to three: \(F_x\), \(F_y\), and the scalar torque \(\tau\).

\[ w = [F; T] \]
\[ w = [F_x, F_y, \tau] \] for planar objects

Why λ Exists

Force and torque have different physical units, so the paper introduces a scaling parameter λ inside the wrench norm. That parameter decides how much one unit of torque should count relative to one unit of force.

\[ \|w\| = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2 + \lambda \tau^2} \]
In the interactive figure below we scale the torque axis directly as s τ, so λ = s^2.

3. How The Quality Definition Really Works

This is the notation-heavy part of the paper, but conceptually it is clean. First define what it means for a generalized force budget to resist a wrench. Then restrict to unit budget. Then ask: over all disturbance directions, what is the smallest reachable boundary distance?

Geometric punchline Quality is a nearest-face distance.

Step A: Feasibility

The predicate \(w A g\) means the generalized contact-force vector \(g\) can produce or resist the wrench \(w\). Think of this as feasibility before optimization.

\[ A = \{ (w, g) \mid w A g \} \]

Step B: Unit Budget

The paper then restricts attention to generalized forces of unit size. That restriction creates the set \(B_G\), which contains every wrench reachable under that normalized budget.

\[ B = \{ (w, g) \mid w A g \text{ and } \|g\| = 1 \} \]
\[ B_G = \bigcup_g B_g \]

Step C: Worst Direction

For one direction, \(LQ_w\) tells you how much wrench magnitude you can obtain per unit force. The global quality \(Q\) is the minimum over directions, because the planner does not control the future disturbance direction.

\[ Q = \min_{\mathrm{dir}(w)} LQ_w \]
\[ Q = \operatorname{distance}(0, \operatorname{Bd}(B_G)) \]

Why This Becomes A Ball Inside A Set

Once everything is phrased in terms of \(B_G\), the paper's famous geometric picture drops out immediately. The quality measure is the radius of the largest ball centered at the origin that fits inside the feasible wrench set.

Positive radius means a positive worst-case disturbance margin.
Zero radius means a supporting face of the feasible set passes through the origin.

4. Why There Are Two Criteria

The paper gives two formulas because there are two physically meaningful notions of "unit finger effort." One cares about the worst single contact load. The other cares about total load summed across contacts. Changing the norm changes the geometry of the feasible wrench set.

The \(L_\infty\) Criterion

Each contact gets its own independent cap. Every contact can spend up to its own local unit effort, so the global feasible set is obtained by combining the local contact sets through a Minkowski sum.

\[ W_G = W_1 \oplus W_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus W_n \]
This is the paper's model for minimizing the maximum finger force.

The \(L_1\) Criterion

All contacts share one total cap. Primitive contact rays therefore compete for the same total budget, so the feasible set becomes the convex hull generated by the primitive wrenches and the origin.

\[ W_G = \operatorname{conv}(0, \text{primitive wrenches}) \]
This is the paper's model for minimizing total finger effort.

What Minkowski Sum Means In This Paper

In Ferrari and Canny, \(W_i\) means the wrench set available from one contact under a local unit budget. In the planar two-edge approximation used here, that local set is the triangle generated by the origin and the two primitive wrench rays at that contact.

\[ W_i = \operatorname{conv}\{0, w_{i,+}, w_{i,-}\} \]
\[ B_G = W_1 \oplus W_2 = \{x_1 + x_2 \mid x_1 \in W_1,\ x_2 \in W_2\} \]

Read that literally: pick one feasible wrench from contact 1, pick one feasible wrench from contact 2, then add them. Under \(L_\infty\), each contact keeps its own cap, so both contacts are allowed to contribute fully at the same time.

That is why Minkowski-sum geometry is usually larger. It is the set of all pairwise sums, not a set of averages. In paper language, this criterion asks the planner to minimize the maximum individual finger load.

What Convex Hull Means In This Paper

Under \(L_1\), the contacts no longer have independent local budgets. All primitive contact rays compete for one shared total effort budget, so the feasible wrench is built by distributing nonnegative weights across primitive wrenches.

\[ B_G = \operatorname{conv}\{0, w_{1,+}, w_{1,-}, w_{2,+}, w_{2,-}\} \]
\[ B_G = \left\{\sum_j \alpha_j w_j \;\middle|\; \alpha_j \ge 0,\ \sum_j \alpha_j \le 1 \right\} \]

Read that literally: you are mixing primitive wrench directions under one shared budget. If one contact spends more of the total effort, the others must spend less.

That is why convex-hull geometry is usually smaller than the Minkowski-sum set. It reflects shared effort, not independent simultaneous spending. In paper language, this criterion asks the planner to minimize total finger effort.

A Worked Example From This Paper's Planar Pinch

Take the symmetric pinch used throughout the demo: contact 1 at the left side, contact 2 at the right side, friction coefficient \(\mu = 1\), and torque scaling \(s = 1\). In that case the four primitive wrenches are

\[ w_{1,+} = [1, 1, -1],\qquad w_{1,-} = [1, -1, 1] \]
\[ w_{2,+} = [-1, -1, -1],\qquad w_{2,-} = [-1, 1, 1] \]

Now compare the same direction under the two criteria. Under \(L_\infty\), each contact may spend its full local budget, so one legal total wrench is

\[ w_{1,+} + w_{2,-} = [1, 1, -1] + [-1, 1, 1] = [0, 2, 0] \]
\[ [0, 2, 0] \in W_1 \oplus W_2 \]

This is the key Minkowski-sum move: choose one feasible point from \(W_1\), choose one feasible point from \(W_2\), and add them. Both contacts contribute fully, so the total wrench can reach farther.

Under \(L_1\), that same point is not available, because the primitive rays must share one unit of total effort. The best you can do in the same direction is a convex combination such as

\[ \tfrac{1}{2} w_{1,+} + \tfrac{1}{2} w_{2,-} = [0, 1, 0] \]
\[ [0, 2, 0] \notin \operatorname{conv}\{0, w_{1,+}, w_{1,-}, w_{2,+}, w_{2,-}\} \]

Why not? Because reaching \([0,2,0]\) would require coefficient \(1\) on \(w_{1,+}\) and coefficient \(1\) on \(w_{2,-}\), so the total coefficient sum would be \(2\), which violates the shared-budget rule. This is exactly why the \(L_\infty\) feasible set is larger than the \(L_1\) feasible set even when both start from the same primitive wrench rays.

Important Consequence

The two criteria are not equivalent. A grasp that is optimal under one can be non-optimal under the other. So whenever the paper switches norms, it is not just changing notation. It is changing the actual search landscape seen by the planner.

5. What The Planning Algorithm Actually Does

Once you accept the wrench-space viewpoint, the algorithm is straightforward: for each candidate grasp geometry, compute primitive contacts, map them to primitive wrenches, build the appropriate feasible set, compute its convex hull, and score the grasp by the distance from the origin to the nearest hull face.

Two-Jaw Polygonal Gripper

  1. Enumerate candidate base and opponent contacts on the polygon.
  2. Turn each contact type into primitive contacts and primitive wrenches.
  3. Build the wrench set implied by the chosen criterion.
  4. Compute the convex hull in wrench space.
  5. Measure the minimum face distance from the origin.
Under the paper's assumptions, this becomes a finite search with linear-time behavior in the number of polygon edges for the two-jaw case.

Three-Jaw Polygonal Gripper

  1. Pick a base side and determine the two opponent contacts induced by the finger triangle.
  2. Reject collision or impossible closure configurations.
  3. Compute primitive contacts and primitive wrenches for the surviving grasp.
  4. Evaluate quality the same way: build the hull and read off the nearest face.
The geometry is more complex, but the scoring principle is identical.

What The Paper Leaves Open

The conclusion points out two open modeling choices: some geometric assumptions are what make the search finite, and force and torque are not naturally comparable, so the wrench norm scaling remains a modeling decision.
